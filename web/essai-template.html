Why does one line have two weights?

Select new trains → safety board. The notebook carries two rows for that directed connection: one records 0.7 and another 0.9. The drawing groups them into one line and the inspector retains the two source rows. Choosing one number or averaging them would add an interpretation that the notebook never supplied.

The line is one of 25 drawn connections. Its single stroke records a pair of endpoints. The original record list is where the disagreement between its weights remains visible.

What changes when the records share one plane?

Switch from Three layers to One plane while keeping the connection selected. New trains remains a scope record, safety board remains a stakeholder, and the recorded direction and both weights stay in the receipt. Their spatial grouping changes. The underlying rows stay in place.

Layered positions make the change of role visible as the arrow crosses the page. The connection index gives the same relationship without requiring the reader to trace that line through the drawing.

What can a project reader take from the arrow?

The source notebook creates a directed network. Looking at a record’s incoming and outgoing links answers different questions, even when the drawing puts their endpoints close together. None of the weights has a documented common unit. The two numbers cannot be read as competing probabilities of approval.

The findings page preserves the reconstruction and the corrections. The commentary keeps the original voice and data conventions so a reader can check where source evidence ends and the present interpretation begins.

What a line means

The original notebook creates a directed network. This edition preserves recorded direction. Reading all connections around a record includes both incoming and outgoing links.

What a weight means

Weights are preserved as entered. Their units and scale are unspecified. Every drawn line has the same width, so a value of 10 is never presented as a stronger claim than a value of 0.9.
